The Jeep Scrambler (CJ-8)
America's Original Off-Road Pickup

Before the Gladiator, before the Comanche — there was the Scrambler. A long-wheelbase CJ with a cargo bed and a cult following that has only grown since production ended.

What Was the Jeep Scrambler?

The Jeep Scrambler — officially the CJ-8 — was produced from 1981 to 1985 by American Motors Corporation (AMC). It was built on a stretched version of the CJ-7 platform, adding 10 inches of wheelbase and a short open cargo bed behind the rear seats. Think of it as the Jeep Wrangler pickup of its era — decades before Jeep would revisit the concept with the Gladiator in 2019.

With only about 27,000 units produced over its five-year run, the Scrambler is now a legitimate collector vehicle. Rust-free examples regularly sell for $15,000–$40,000 or more, with restored or modified builds commanding even higher prices.

Why so rare? The Scrambler was discontinued in 1985 when AMC shifted focus to the Wrangler (YJ) platform. Its short production window and the fact that many were used hard off-road means clean survivors are increasingly difficult to find.
  • 1981AMC introduces the CJ-8 Scrambler — a CJ-7 stretched 10 inches with an open cargo bed. Available with inline-4, inline-6, or V8 engines.
  • 1982Minor updates. The Scrambler finds a niche with buyers who want Jeep capability and light truck utility in one package.
  • 1983–84Continued production with incremental improvements. Off-road use keeps Scramblers popular in the Southwest and mountain states.
  • 1985Final year of production. AMC discontinues the CJ-8 as it prepares the new YJ Wrangler platform. Approximately 27,000 total units built.
  • TodayThe Scrambler is a growing collector vehicle. Its combination of Jeep DNA, pickup utility, and rarity make it one of the most desirable vintage 4x4s in America.

Engine Options

The CJ-8 Scrambler was offered with three engine options depending on year:

  • 2.5L 4-cylinder (151 CID) — base engine, adequate but not beloved
  • 4.2L inline-6 (258 CID) — the most popular choice, torquey and reliable
  • 5.0L V8 (304 CID) — rare option, desirable for performance builds

The 4.2L inline-6 is widely considered the best stock powertrain for trail use — it's the same basic engine that powered Jeeps for decades and has an enormous aftermarket support base.

Interior & Seat Covers

The Scrambler's interior is simple CJ-era utilitarian: vinyl seats, basic gauges, no creature comforts. The front bucket seats are shared with the CJ-7. The rear area behind the seats is a small jump-seat space, with the cargo bed starting just aft of the B-pillar.

Because the Scrambler was built in such small numbers, there are no seat covers manufactured specifically for the CJ-8. Your options for seat cover upgrades are:

  • Custom upholstery — the best route for show-quality builds. Local upholstery shops familiar with vintage Jeeps can replicate factory or build custom.
  • CJ-7 compatible covers — the front seats are identical to the CJ-7, so covers made for the CJ-7 will fit the Scrambler's front buckets.
  • Universal-fit covers — for a working trail rig, universal neoprene or canvas covers sized for compact buckets will work.

Roll Bar & Grab Handle Accessories

Like all CJ-era Jeeps, the Scrambler runs an exposed roll bar — and grip on that bar matters on the trail. The Scrambler's roll bar accepts standard CJ-diameter grab handles, and paracord grab handles are a popular upgrade for both function and appearance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Jeep Scrambler the same as the Jeep Gladiator?

No — but they share the same concept. The Scrambler (CJ-8) was a CJ-based open-top pickup built 1981–1985. The Jeep Gladiator (JT) is the modern JL Wrangler-based pickup introduced in 2019. Both are Jeep pickup trucks, but they share no mechanical parts and were built 34 years apart.

How many Jeep Scramblers were built?

Approximately 27,000 CJ-8 Scramblers were produced across the five model years (1981–1985). This relatively small number — combined with decades of off-road use and rust — makes clean examples increasingly rare and valuable.

What seat covers fit a Jeep Scrambler?

The CJ-8 Scrambler uses the same front bucket seats as the CJ-7. Any seat cover designed for the CJ-7 front seats will fit. There are no seat covers manufactured specifically for the CJ-8. Universal-fit covers sized for compact buckets are a practical option for trail use.

Are Jeep Scrambler parts hard to find?

Less hard than you'd expect. Because the Scrambler shares most of its mechanicals with the CJ-7, parts availability is much better than for truly rare vehicles. The AMC 258 inline-6, Dana 30/44 axles, T4/T5 transmissions — all have strong aftermarket support. Body panels and Scrambler-specific trim pieces are harder to source.

What is a Jeep Scrambler worth today?

Values have risen significantly in recent years. Rough project Scramblers start around $5,000–$8,000. Driver-quality examples typically sell for $12,000–$22,000. Restored, low-mile, or particularly well-built trail rigs can exceed $35,000–$45,000 depending on the build.
